\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why there was no crossover}

The idea of a crossover rests on an asymmetry: a monolith can only be scaled as a whole, so at some load the
ability to give more resources to just the overloaded part should win. Under an equal budget that asymmetry runs
the other way. The budget is fixed, so decomposition cannot add resources; it can only divide them. Dividing them
had three measured consequences. Every request did more work in total: \xo{perhop/browse/B/cpu}--%
\xo{perhop/browse/C/cpu}\,ms of CPU per remote call, which for checkout in~C raised the CPU per request from
\xo{cpums/checkout/A/400/total} to \xo{cpums/checkout/C/400/total}\,ms. The work was spread unevenly: the edge service needed several times the CPU of the
others, yet received the same share, while the databases and the modules a workload did not touch kept part of
the budget idle. And the smallest shares were the first to suffer from CFS throttling, which made tail latency
erratic at average utilisations of 70--85\%, well below the nominal limit. The monolith's single process, by
contrast, could use the whole application budget for whichever module was busy, and its capacity was set by its
database.

None of this means that decomposition cannot win on performance. It means that its performance case depends on
using resources the monolith cannot use: a larger total budget, or one reallocated as load shifts. Berry et
al.\ found microservices ahead of a monolith without replication and the outcome dependent on the scaling
strategy~\cite{Berry2024}, and Blinowski et al.\ found that scaling out beyond a certain number of instances degraded
performance~\cite{Blinowski2022}. Our results add a way to put a number on the entry price. The extra CPU per
request (Table~\ref{tab:cost}) is the capacity a decomposed deployment must recover through better allocation
before it breaks even; on this testbed it is roughly 0.2--0.3\,ms of CPU per hop without a mesh and
\xo{perhop/browse/D/cpu}--\xo{perhop/checkout/D/cpu}\,ms with one.

\subsection{Implications for practitioners}

\paragraph{Price each synchronous hop.} At low load a remote call cost \xo{perhop/browse/C/p50}--%
\xo{perhop/checkout/B/p50}\,ms of median latency and about \xo{perhop/browse/C/net}--\xo{perhop/browse/B/net}\,ms of
transit here, and most of it was not serialisation. These figures come from one environment and one stack, but
the method that produced them is cheap: a \texttt{Server-Timing} header and a load generator that records it.
A team can measure its own per-hop price before deciding how finely to cut.

\paragraph{Size the edge service, not the service count.} The capacity of every decomposed configuration was the
capacity of its edge service's CPU share. A deployment that splits a budget evenly will hit that limit early, and
CFS throttling will show up in the tail before it shows up in average utilisation. Monitoring the throttled-period
counter of the edge service is a better early warning than its CPU utilisation.

\paragraph{Account for the mesh's CPU.} On a sidecar mesh the proxies used more CPU per request than the
application itself. Their latency cost, a few tenths of a millisecond per hop, was modest; their CPU cost was not.
Sidecar-less data planes are reported to reduce the overhead substantially~\cite{BremlerBarr2025} and should be
measured in the same way before they are adopted for performance reasons.

\paragraph{Prefer coarse decomposition when decomposing for other reasons.} Organisational and deployment
benefits are legitimate reasons to split a system~\cite{Su2024}. When they are the reason, our data favour the
coarsest split that achieves them: B reached \xo{xmaxratio/browse/B}--\xo{xmaxratio/checkout/B} of the monolith's
capacity, C and D about a tenth or less.

\subsection{The protocol, revisited}

Building the protocol changed it. The original latency decomposition subtracted span timestamps from different
hosts and therefore depended on clock synchronisation; the \texttt{Server-Timing} accounting does not, and it
assigns every millisecond to exactly one layer. The original planned to fit the USL to open-loop data, a model of a
closed system applied to an open one; the study now uses each load model for what it measures. The original
Config~D changed two variables at once. And several defaults of the software stack turned out to matter for a fair
comparison: the garbage collector the JVM selects under small CPU limits, per-pod memory limits that are too small
for a JVM, unbounded concurrency that turns overload into OOM kills, and keep-alive races between client and server.
These are reported because others building such testbeds will meet them.

\subsection{Future work}

The same campaign runner targets a bare-metal cluster and GKE; running it there separates the effects of the
single-host environment from those of the architectures and quantifies the cloud network's share of the
\emph{net} layer. Two treatments follow directly from the results: a demand-proportional CPU allocation, and the
Horizontal Pod Autoscaler with a cluster-wide cap, the setting in which a crossover is most plausible. Further
variants are gRPC instead of JSON over HTTP/1.1, a sidecar-less mesh, and a modular monolith as a fifth
configuration.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We compared a monolith with three decompositions of identical code under an equal CPU budget, measured with
open-loop and closed-loop load, a per-layer latency accounting that needs no synchronised clocks, and cgroup
counters of every container. Decomposition added latency at low load: the p99 of the decomposed configurations was
\xo{ratio/browse/B/50/est}--\xo{ratio/checkout/D/25/est} times the monolith's. It reduced capacity to between about
a quarter and a sixteenth of the monolith's, because the budget was divided among services that did not need it
equally and the edge service hit its CPU quota first. A sidecar mesh added
\xo{mesh/browse/200/perhop}--\xo{mesh/checkout/25/perhop}\,ms per hop and more CPU per request than the application.
Under these conditions there is no load at which decomposition pays off; its performance case requires resources or
elasticity beyond an equal share. The testbed, data and analysis are published so that the same measurements can be
repeated on other infrastructure and with other allocation policies.
