The cloud campaign repeated the main comparison on Amazon EKS: \xo{eks/cells} fresh deployments over
\xo{eks/hours}\,h, with every response again byte-identical to the monolith's (\xo{eks/equiv/match} of
\xo{eks/equiv/total}). Table~\ref{tab:env} sets its results beside the local ones. The verdicts are the same:
H1 is supported, H2 is falsified, H3 is supported, and the bottleneck bound names the same container in every
configuration, the monolith's database and the edge service of each decomposed one.

The magnitudes differ, in two directions. The latency penalty at low load is larger in the cloud. The monolith
was faster there, with a browse p99 of \xo{eks/p99/browse/A/50}\,ms at 50 requests per second against
\xo{p99/browse/A/50}\,ms locally, because the Graviton nodes do not show the local host's wake-up penalty. Every
remote call, by contrast, now crossed a real network, and the p99 ratios at the lowest rate were
\xo{eks/h1/min}--\xo{eks/h1/max}. Transit per hop, \xo{eks/perhop/browse/net/min}--\xo{eks/perhop/browse/net/max}\,ms
on browse, was close to the local value. A sidecar pair cost about twice as much as locally,
\xo{eks/H3/per_hop_shallow_ms}--\xo{eks/H3/per_hop_deep_ms}\,ms per hop, and the mesh accounted for
\xo{eks/H3/share_deep}\% of end-to-end latency on the six-hop path.

The capacity gap, in turn, is smaller. The scheduler placed the monolith's database on a different node from its
application in every repetition, so every query crossed the network, and the database saturated at
\xo{eks/peak/browse/A} browse and \xo{eks/peak/checkout/A} checkout requests per second. The decomposed
configurations reached \xo{eks/peakpct/min}--\xo{eks/peakpct/max}\% of these peaks, against
\xo{peakpct/min}--\xo{peakpct/max}\% locally. The generator's dispatch lag stayed below
\xo{eks/lagmax/checkout}\,ms at the 99th percentile, so the cloud's low-rate measurements are also free of the
local generator's burstiness.
