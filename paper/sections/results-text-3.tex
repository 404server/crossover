

\subsection{Where the time goes (RQ3)}
\label{sec:res-layers}

Figure~\ref{fig:layers} breaks the mean latency at the lowest rate into the six layers. In the monolith most
of it is database time (\xo{layer/browse/A/50/db} of \xo{mean/browse/A/50}\,ms for browse,
\xo{layer/checkout/A/25/db} of \xo{mean/checkout/A/25}\,ms for checkout); application code, serialisation and
framework dispatch together take a few tenths of a millisecond. Decomposition leaves database time roughly where
it was (browse: \xo{layer/browse/B/50/db}\,ms in~B and \xo{layer/browse/C/50/db}\,ms in~C) and adds inter-service
transit on top: \xo{layer/browse/B/50/net} and \xo{layer/browse/C/50/net}\,ms for browse in B and~C,
\xo{layer/checkout/B/25/net} and \xo{layer/checkout/C/25/net}\,ms for checkout. Per hop this is
\xo{perhop/browse/net/min}--\xo{perhop/browse/net/max}\,ms for browse and \xo{perhop/checkout/net/min}--\xo{perhop/checkout/net/max}\,ms for checkout. At low load this includes the host's wake-up penalty; at the highest
rate all configurations sustained, a hop cost \xo{perhop/browse/nethigh/min}--\xo{perhop/browse/nethigh/max}\,ms on
browse.

The tail is made of the same layer. The p99 is set by the slowest 1\% of requests, and in those requests, too, the
difference to the monolith lies in transit. On browse at the lowest rate, the slowest 1\% of B's requests were
\xo{taildiff/browse/B/tot}\,ms slower than the monolith's slowest 1\%, while their transit alone was
\xo{taildiff/browse/B/net}\,ms longer; database, application and edge time were slightly shorter. In~C the figures
are \xo{taildiff/browse/C/tot} and \xo{taildiff/browse/C/net}\,ms, and on checkout in~C \xo{taildiff/checkout/C/tot}
and \xo{taildiff/checkout/C/net}\,ms. Serialisation, which fleet-scale studies count among the costs every request pays~\cite{Kanev2015}, adds only
\xo{perhop/browse/D/ser}--\xo{perhop/checkout/B/ser}\,ms per hop for these payloads (at most about 5.5\,kB of JSON).
In D the transit layer nearly doubles and database time rises, because every query now also passes
through two sidecars (Section~\ref{sec:res-mesh}).

The decomposed configurations also cost more to run. Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports CPU time per request at the
highest rate all four sustained. For browse at \xo{ref/browse/high} requests per second the monolith spent
\xo{cpumsref/browse/A/total}\,ms of CPU per request, B \xo{cpumsref/browse/B/total}, C
\xo{cpumsref/browse/C/total} and D \xo{cpumsref/browse/D/total}\,ms; for checkout at \xo{ref/checkout/high} the
figures are \xo{cpumsref/checkout/A/total}, \xo{cpumsref/checkout/B/total}, \xo{cpumsref/checkout/C/total} and
\xo{cpumsref/checkout/D/total}\,ms. Each remote call added \xo{perhop/cpu/nomesh/min}--\xo{perhop/cpu/nomesh/max}\,ms of
CPU in B and~C and \xo{perhop/cpu/mesh/min}--\xo{perhop/cpu/mesh/max}\,ms in~D. The resident memory of all pods
grew from \xo{memref/browse/A}\,GiB for the monolith to \xo{memref/browse/B}, \xo{memref/browse/C} and
\xo{memref/browse/D}\,GiB, mostly the fixed footprint of one JVM per service. The database tier's CPU per request
rose more slowly, from \xo{cpumsref/browse/A/db} to \xo{cpumsref/browse/D/db}\,ms for browse; most of the extra
cost is in the application and mesh tiers.
